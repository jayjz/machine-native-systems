2. What supports—and challenges—the hypothesis

Supporting evidence:

    TEMPER’s September 17 result reports BERT validation macro F1 of 0.9539, versus 0.8864 for TF-IDF/logistic regression. That demonstrates specialist feasibility on one fixed intent task. It does not demonstrate calibrated deployment or superiority to a general model; I did not recompute the external prediction artifacts.
    SHAD0W and
    AetherForge express freshness, permissions, resource limits, and lifecycle transitions directly. Those operations have no intrinsic need for generated prose.
    FrugalGPT and RouteLLM report benchmark-specific advantages from selective computation. Anthropic’s engineering guidance explicitly allows conventional classifiers for routing. Much of this architecture already has precedent. 

Counterevidence and limitations:

    Language-mediated deliberation can improve decisions. ReAct demonstrated benefits from interleaving reasoning and environmental interaction. Removing that computation may reduce useful adaptability.
    Your comparative evidence remains incomplete: TEMPER has no demonstrated full-cost cascade advantage under shift; TraceForge’s two-case baseline tests evidence handling; Fracture’s inspected core runtime still raises NotImplementedError.
    CipherLoop’s validator assigns confidence 0.9 as a constant. An AST trace provides bounded analysis evidence; it does not establish calibrated confidence or exploitability.
    Misleading explanations are documented, but recent ACL 2026 work challenges treating omitted hints alone as proof that reasoning is unfaithful. Explanation can be useful without being sufficient evidence. 

I cannot verify the broad claim that industry systems generally overuse LLMs. The inspected evidence supports particular replacement opportunities, not their prevalence.

3. Where replacement makes architectural sense

From first principles, a decision needs relevant information, an objective, constraints, and a policy. Conversation is one way to compute that policy.
Operation	Plausible replacement
Permissions, freshness, budgets	Predicates, policy engines, capabilities
Known retries and lifecycle transitions	State machines, durable workflows
Fixed semantic labels	Classifiers or encoders with abstention
Planning over explicit transitions	Search, constraint solving, optimization
Hidden-state estimation	Bayesian filters, probabilistic programs, POMDPs
Resource adaptation	Feedback control and admission control
Correctness checks	Tests, static analysis, formal verifiers, external observations

OPA and Temporal already provide policy evaluation and durable state reconstruction mechanisms respectively.

General-purpose models retain their strongest case when the relevant state must first be discovered: unfamiliar code, ambiguous requirements, changing concepts, open-ended investigation, and synthesis. Their necessity must be tested against alternatives.

Three independent choices are often conflated: typed interfaces, bounded cognition, and enforced authority. An LLM can satisfy the first and operate beneath the third. Structured Outputs still permit substantive mistakes; final-output guardrails cannot substitute for authorization before an external effect.

4. Terminology worth adopting

    Sufficient state representation: does the schema preserve everything needed for the decision?
    Selective risk / coverage: error among accepted decisions, and the fraction accepted.
    Learning to defer / value of information: when escalation improves expected outcomes enough to justify its cost.
    Decision-focused learning: optimize downstream consequences rather than label accuracy alone.
    Complete mediation / TOCTOU: every action crosses enforcement; permission may become stale before execution.
    Verifier soundness and completeness: false acceptance and false rejection are distinct failures.

Calibration, selective prediction, and conformal risk control are established research areas. Their guarantees depend on assumptions; they do not provide unrestricted protection against distribution shift.

5. Three decisive experiments

    Is prose deliberation needed for a fixed semantic decision?
    Extend TEMPER: compare specialist, constrained-label general model, deliberative general model, and specialist-plus-escalation. Keep authority and evidence identical. Test untouched data plus out-of-scope cases. Predeclare acceptable error, minimum coverage, and full-cost savings. Falsification: savings disappear after maintenance/escalation, coverage collapses, or risk increases under shift.

    Does independent authority help regardless of intelligence?
    In a fake-broker harness, cross rule/model proposer × advisory/enforced gate. Inject stale state, duplicates, crashes, lost acknowledgements, and persuasive bypass requests. Measure illegal effects and useful legal completions. Falsification: enforcement permits prohibited effects or achieves safety mainly by blocking useful work. Benefits shared across proposers establish an authority insight, not model-replacement superiority.

    Does typed state discard consequential information?
    Construct security cases with identical typed summaries but different omitted context requiring different decisions. Compare state-only decisions, full-context decisions, and evidence-triggered escalation. Falsification: full context reliably resolves distinctions the schema collapses, while escalation cannot recover them economically. This directly tests the abstraction rather than rewarding determinism.

Freeze protocols before evaluation and report confidence intervals. None of these experiments has been completed by this investigation.

6. The single unresolved question

Can a compact, maintainable state representation preserve enough information for useful decisions under novelty—without moving the intelligence and cost into extraction, verification, escalation, and maintenance?

@Deepresearch

Investigate from first principles how intelligent machine components should communicate and coordinate to form reliable, coherent systems.

Start from this premise, but do not assume it is correct:

Intelligence, authority, verification, memory, execution, and explanation may be separate system roles. If so, natural-language messages between general-purpose agents may not be the ideal internal communication substrate.

The central question is:

What is the best machine-native interface for composing heterogeneous intelligent components into a coherent system?

Research the current state of the field through October 2026. Prioritize peer-reviewed research, arXiv, major systems conferences, formal methods/control literature, and official technical documentation. Include recent agent architectures, but do not limit the investigation to LLM-agent literature.

Compare mechanisms including:

* natural-language agent messages;
* typed state and structured outputs;
* event streams and event sourcing;
* state machines and durable workflows;
* blackboard architectures;
* actor/message-passing systems;
* shared-memory systems;
* belief/state representations;
* probabilistic messages and confidence distributions;
* constraint and capability systems;
* contracts and protocol/state-machine types;
* planners and search;
* verifier/proposer architectures;
* control-theoretic and robotics architectures;
* multi-agent protocols.

Distinguish carefully between communication, coordination, reasoning, authority, and evidence.

Investigate whether current LLM-agent systems are rediscovering older ideas from distributed systems, robotics, control theory, multi-agent systems, formal methods, and software architecture—and identify what LLMs genuinely change.

Use SHAD0W, TEMPER, CipherLoop, TraceForge, AetherForge, and Fracture from my GitHub as examples of the architectural questions motivating this investigation, not as evidence that the hypothesis is correct.

I specifically want to know:

1. What information actually needs to cross boundaries between machine components?
2. When should that information be prose versus typed state, events, probabilities, constraints, capabilities, or evidence?
3. How should components communicate uncertainty?
4. How should authority and permission propagate—or deliberately not propagate?
5. How can a system remain coherent when components use fundamentally different kinds of intelligence?
6. Where does shared context help, and where does it create hidden coupling?
7. What properties should a machine-native protocol have for auditability, recoverability, composability, and independent verification?
8. What existing architectures come closest to this?
9. What important research already falsifies or weakens this direction?

Do not propose a product yet.

Produce a research map that identifies the established fields, strongest prior art, unresolved research gaps, and contradictions.

End by answering one question:

If we were designing intelligent machine systems today without inheriting the chatbot metaphor, what would their internal communication architecture look like—and what evidence supports that design?
